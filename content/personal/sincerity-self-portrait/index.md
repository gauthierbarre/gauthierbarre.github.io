---
title: "Does sincerity have the same meaning for Montaigne, Rousseau and Stendhal?"
summary: A short essay on sincerity in self-portrayal, and why the process matters more than the final portrait.
date: 2026-11-17
---

Sincerity appears at different layers. When one tries to describe oneself, from restituting to interpreting and communicating, sincerity is important at each step to reduce bias. Each author insists on and highlights different aspects of this sincerity. The authors use sincerity to claim truth; thus, a tension appears when the author is both the object of analysis and the one who analyses. Therefore, are all forms of sincerity equally relevant in self-representation?

Montaigne insists on the sincerity of the writing process, claiming to write "in good faith", but gives less importance to the analysis of himself, saying "I want to be seen". Rousseau insists on the sincerity of the analysis, claiming "I have shown myself as I was", but his wish to show himself as unique may introduce bias into his writing. Stendhal insists on the rigor of recalling his memories, but his first impressions and his difficulty in knowing himself show that his analysis may be biased. A common point is that all three authors praise sincerity as a way to reach the truth, but this raises a problem.

Sincerity, in the case of these authors, is used to show the truthfulness of their writing, but sincerity can be irrelevant when it is impossible for them to reach the truth. First, the person analysing and the object being analysed are the same, which makes it difficult to avoid bias, even with sincerity, especially when the process is done alone. Indeed, as exposed by Plato in *Gorgias*, dialogue with others and questioning are more important than sincerity alone to reach the truth. Therefore, sincerity in writing is not enough to reach the truth; what matters is the process used to reach it. For instance, in Stendhal's text, we perceive much more of his personality and who he was, even though he does not directly try to describe himself.

All three authors recognise some of their biases, but the important point is not simply to present these biases, it is to show how they try to approach reality. Rousseau claims to have done a work that nobody has ever done, but does not really explain the process of his analysis. Montaigne gives little explanation of how he reaches this representation. Stendhal shows more of his process, questioning his memories, his character and even his ability to write sincerely. This sincerity of the process is the most relevant, because the authors learn who they are through it, while the outside reader can understand them better by seeing how they reached their conclusions.

Sincerity is therefore not exactly the same in the three authors, but they share the desire to represent themselves truthfully. However, the path towards the representation is more important and contains more truth about themselves than the final representation. Therefore, sincerity throughout the whole process is far more important than sincerity at specific steps to prove that what one says is true. To go further, Pablo Picasso painted self-portraits that were not necessarily realistic representations of himself, but still revealed something about him through his choice of colours, his artistic decisions and the whole process of creation. Rather than seeking to convince us at each step that his representation reflects reality, it is through the sincerity of the whole creative process that a deeper truth about himself can emerge.
