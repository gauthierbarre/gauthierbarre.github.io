Systematic equity · MaQI Hackathon 1 · 2026

Four Signals on the US Top 500, Net of Costs

Repository ↗

Problem

Do four classic cross-sectional signals — mean-reversion, momentum, quality and value — still earn money on US large caps once realistic trading costs are paid? And can a signal that predicts well but trades too much be made profitable?

Methodology

  • Point-in-time universe of the 500 largest US stocks, re-selected each year end (no survivorship bias), 2011–2024; fundamentals enter on their filing date.
  • Data cleaning of documented loopholes: splits, dividends and spin-offs in total return, frozen prices, negative book equity, issue- vs company-level share counts.
  • Signal diagnostics with rank information coefficients (Spearman) across horizons and decile returns.
  • Quintile long-short books, 10% volatility target, execution the day after the signal, linear costs of 2, 5 and 10 bp; turnover and break-even cost per signal.
  • Robustness by sub-period and cost level, plus a live-monitoring design: realised vol, rolling IC, cost ratio, drawdown and sector exposure, each with a threshold and a decision.

Takeaways

Slow signals (momentum, quality) survive costs; value lost money even gross over this decade; mean-reversion predicts but its gross edge (≈1.2 bp per unit traded) sits below the cheapest cost. Rebalancing it every 4 days halves turnover and turns the 2 bp Sharpe positive — a frequency chosen in-sample, so flagged as fragile. With 14 years of data the Sharpe standard error is ≈0.27: even momentum is not statistically significant, which the project states explicitly.

Index research · MaQI Hackathon · 1990–2026

S&P 500 Composition: Rules, Reproduction, Prediction

Repository ↗

Problem

What actually decides which companies enter and leave the S&P 500? Can the month-end composition be rebuilt well enough to reproduce the published total-return index, and can it be predicted past the data vendor's cutoff, both at t and h months ahead?

Methodology

  • Rules measured from membership spells: cap rank of additions and deletions, delisting after exit, turnover by year, and the methodology's entry screens rebuilt point in time (cap threshold, four positive quarters, liquidity).
  • Index reconstruction: daily total returns weighted by previous-day cap on the exact membership, benchmarked against monthly and equal-weight builds; survivorship-free returns.
  • Prediction at t: rule-based recursion from the last observed composition, filling slots by sector share of large-cap value then by cap; frozen at 1996-12 and tested out of sample on 1997–2001.
  • Prediction at t+h: entry and exit classifiers (logistic regression, gradient boosting) on size, liquidity, momentum, fundamentals and tenure, compared with persistence from 1 to 60 months.
  • Scoring with overlap, additions/deletions recall and precision, Brier score, ROC AUC; correlation, slope and tracking error for index checks.

Takeaways

Membership is not a rank cut: additions enter at a median cap rank of 575, and in a given month 54% of members rank outside the top 500. Persistence is a hard baseline (overlap 0.9932 at one month, 0.7409 at five years); the recursion catches additions persistence cannot, at the cost of overlap. The README documents the limitations openly — missing monthly caps before 1998, today's GICS used for sector balancing, no float data after 2005.

About & Contact

I am interested in the parts of quantitative finance where careful empirical work makes the difference: how a universe is defined, what was knowable at each date, what a strategy really costs to trade, and whether a result beats a simple baseline once its standard error is taken into account.

My recent work was developed during MaQI hackathons on licensed S&P Global data: a study of four equity factors net of costs, and a reconstruction and forecast of S&P 500 membership over 1990–2026. I am looking for opportunities in quantitative research and quantitative development.
